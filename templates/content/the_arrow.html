I had started taking walks when I stopped answering prayers. Not that there were many addressed to Aphrodite's little helper anymore.

It was an easy habit to pick up. The gods rarely noticed where I went, as long as I returned when they called. And if they did notice, they had more important things to worry about than one of their lesser inconveniences wandering along the shore.

I preferred it that way.

There was a path between the cliffs and the sea, far enough from the temples that no one came looking for me. I walked it most evenings. Sometimes I carried my bow.

That day, I didn't.

I was thinking about nothing in particular. That was the point.

But the thoughts came anyway.

The last time Aphrodite had summoned me, she had spoken my name as though it were a command.

Make him love her.

Make her love him.

Make them forget.

Make them want.

I had begun to wonder how many lives could be ruined by something as small as an arrow and as large as a god's ego.

— They like to say that love begins with a wound, a woman spoke beside me.

I stopped.

She was sitting on a low stone overlooking the water. I hadn't seen her there. She didn't look at me.

— A little blood, she continued, a little light, a god with a bow pulled tight between his fingers.

The hair on the back of my neck stood up.

I had no idea whether she knew who I was. Most mortals didn't recognize me. Not without the bow.

She smiled faintly.

— They like the story better that way. Sudden. Almost beautiful.

For a moment, I wondered if she was talking to herself. Then she turned her head. Her eyes met mine.

I almost walked away, instead, I sat down at the other end of the stone. There was something about her voice that I couldn't place.

Not the sound of it. The certainty.

— Do you believe it? she asked.

— Believe what?

— That love begins with a wound.

I gave a small laugh.

— Pff. Poetry.

Then I looked at her.

— I think people say things like that when they want someone else to blame.

She laughed quietly.

— Well, when something goes wrong, there must be someone to blame.

I almost smiled.

The woman picked up a small stone and turned it between her fingers.

— Blame is simple. Responsibility, though…

She paused.

— Ah. That one is difficult, I said.

She glanced at me.

— And blame isn't? You sound as if you've given this some thought.

— Too much.

Mortals wanted someone to blame when a marriage failed, when a lover left, when desire became resentment.

Gods wanted someone to blame when their games became inconvenient. And sometimes they blamed me.

We sat in silence for a while.

The sea was unusually calm.

— Do you think people choose who they love? she asked.

The question should have been harmless, instead, I thought of Colchis.

A young woman standing among strangers. Dark hair, a frightened face and prince who had come from across the sea. He was standing before her father, asking for something no one had any intention of giving him.

The golden fleece.

And then there was my arrow. I remembered it disappearing beneath her skin.

I remembered her hands gathering the plants.

The herbs she knew how to use. The instructions she whispered to him.

The way she watched him as if he were already the answer to a question she had been asking all her life.

She was already lost. Or perhaps I had made sure she would be.

Back then, I thought it was just a game, another little trick, another play I could give an unexpected turn.

I pushed the memory away.

— Sometimes, I said.

— And the rest of the time?

— The rest of the time they tell themselves they had no choice. That it was the right thing to do. The only thing they could do.

She laughed softly.

— That sounds cruel.

— It is.

— Is it true?

I looked at her.

— I don't know.

She lowered her eyes to the stone in her hand.

— Perhaps that's the cruelest part.

Cruel. That was a strong word. I had never thought of myself as cruel. Not even when Aphrodite sent me to meddle in people's lives.

I had thought of myself as useful. There was a difference. At least, there used to be.

The past had found another way to catch up with me. I saw the prince again.

Jason.

He had stood before the king with demands so bold they bordered on arrogance. Ha! Did he really think he could simply ask for the fleece and be given it?

Instead, the king gave him tasks no ordinary man could complete.

Fire-breathing bulls, a field to plough and seeds that would grow into armed men.

Impossible things. And impossible have always attracted those who have something to prove. Mortals. Gods.

The girl.

She knew how to make the impossible merely difficult.

I remembered the way she helped him survive each task and then the fleece was his.

He had done it and was leaving with the girl.

She had chosen him.

Or had she? I could never decide which part of that memory bothered me more.

Women on the cliff said, almost casually,

— Have you ever regretted something you did because you believed you were only doing what you were supposed to do?

I looked out at the sea. There was no reason for the memory to return, and yet it did.

Jason holding the golden fleece and the girl standing beside him.

— No, I said.

The lie surprised me. She didn't call me on it, only smiled.

— Neither have I.

For some reason, I believed her. Perhaps because she didn't sound like someone who was trying to convince me.

She let the silence settle between us, then she tossed the stone into the sea.

— Do you still do it?

— Do what?

— What you were told.

I looked at her. She shrugged.

— Whatever it is you do.

I almost laughed.

— You assume I do something.

— Don't you?

— Not anymore.

— Why?

The question was so simple that I didn't know how to answer it.

Because I was tired.

Because I had begun to hate the sound of my own name when it was followed by an order.

Because after centuries of watching mortals suffer the consequences of things they had never asked for, I had started to wonder what right I had to keep making choices for them.

Instead, I said,

— I got tired of being useful.

She looked at me for a moment.

— If you're not useful, then what are you?

I looked down at my hands.

— I don't know.

She smiled.

— That must be frightening.

— It is.

— And freeing?

I thought about that.

— No.

She seemed surprised.

— I thought freedom was supposed to feel good.

— Maybe it does when you've had it before.

She was quiet.

I wondered if I had said too much.

The wind carried the smell of salt across the path.

— Perhaps, she said, we only notice freedom after someone has taken it away.

I looked at her. There was something familiar in the way she said it. The weight behind the words.

It reminded me of the girl again and what came after. The loss she suffered. The blood she spilled.

The shore of Colchis growing smaller behind them. Her father somewhere back there and her little brother too. Oh, the poor boy. How young he had been? Too young to understand what was happening. And it happened so quickly as if she hadn't given it a thought.

Blood on the deck.

A body in the dark.

I had never thought about that part for long. It was easier not to. The girl had wanted to leave, and so she did.

She had chosen.

And somehow, each choice seemed to take her further from the person she had been before I ever drew my bow.

I had always remembered the arrow.

I had never remembered what came after it.

— What did it cost her? I whispered.

The woman looked at me.

— What?

I realized I had spoken aloud.

— Nothing.

The woman said nothing for a while. Neither of us moved.

Then she asked,

— Do you think love can survive a wrong beginning?

I should have asked what she meant, instead, I thought of a ship.

— I suppose it depends on what you call wrong.

— And what do you call it?

I looked at the sea.

— A choice that wasn't yours.

She was silent for so long that I turned to look at her. She was watching me. Not accusingly, almost sadly. My hands went still.

The ship was gone, another memory took its place. A house, children and life that looked, from a distance, almost ordinary. And for a while, perhaps, she had been happy until Jason came home with news.

He was going to marry another woman.

He had reasons. He spoke of status, security, the future and of her - a foreign girl who would never truly be accepted by his people.

He spoke as if the life they had built together was something he could simply leave behind.

As if there were no other choice.

— And what if, after all that, they decided they wanted someone else? the woman beside me said.

Something tightened in my chest.

— They had a choice too, I said.

— Yes.

She looked at me.

— That is the problem.

I didn't understand. Or perhaps I understood too well.

— Why?

— Because once you have been told that your whole life began with someone else's choice, it becomes very difficult to know which parts of it belong to you.

I looked away. I thought of Jason and the arrow.

And then I remembered what happened after he left.

The memory was not clean, it came in pieces.

Another woman dressed in white. A gift sent by a wife who had been rejected. A beautiful dress and a crown.

For a moment, everything looked almost peaceful.

Then the fire came. I remembered the screams of the bride and her father reaching for her.

The flames.

I had not been there, but I had heard the story. Everyone had.

— She must have hated him, I said.

— Perhaps.

— Enough to do all that?

The woman looked at the sea.

— Perhaps hatred is not always the beginning of a terrible thing.

I looked at her.

— What is, then?

She didn't answer, I already knew.

Love.

The memory shifted again. The house was quieter this time, too quiet.

I remembered a child laughing, small footsteps, a hand reaching for someone.

Then nothing.

I closed my eyes. I had never seen what happened. That was what I told myself.

I had only fired the arrow.

I had not told her to leave Colchis.

I had not told her to betray her father.

I had not told her to follow Jason.

I had not told Jason to abandon her.

And I certainly had not told her what to do after that. I had only made her love him. The thought had always been enough to make me feel innocent.

Now it wasn't. I opened my eyes.

The woman was still sitting beside me. She had been listening, or perhaps remembering.

— Do you think a person can become responsible for a choice they never truly had? she asked.

I looked at the sea. The question struck deeper this time.

— Eventually, I said.

The word tasted bitter. She said nothing. Neither did I. The sun had almost disappeared behind the water. I looked at her again and suddenly the memories stopped feeling like separate things.

The girl running from her father.

The wife Jason left behind.

The woman who sent the poisoned gifts.

The mother in the silent house.

I looked at the woman sitting beside me.

For the first time, I noticed how still she had been throughout our conversation.

She wasn't asking because she wanted to know the story. She already knew it. She was asking because she wanted to know what I would do with it.

— What is your name? I asked.

She looked at me with a sad smile. For a moment, neither of us spoke.

— You know.

And I did.

I remembered.

Medea.

The name rose in my mind like something I had spent years trying to bury. She looked back at the sea.

— If you hadn't fired the arrow, she said, do you think my life would have been different?

I opened my mouth. Nothing came.
